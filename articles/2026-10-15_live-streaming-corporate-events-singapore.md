# Live Streaming a Corporate Event in Singapore: A Practical Guide

**Short answer:** A reliable corporate live stream needs five things:

- a **wired internet connection** with a **mobile backup**
- **clean audio from the venue's AV desk**
- **slides and speakers combined** into one picture
- a **full rehearsal** before doors open
- **someone looking after the online audience**: chat, Q&A and technical help

Start by deciding the platform and how online viewers will take part. That decides the crew and setup you need.

## First decide what kind of stream you need

| Type | What it looks like | Typical setup |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple broadcast** | One camera on the stage, slides shown separately or not at all | 1 camera, 1 operator |
| **Produced stream** | Switching between wide shot, speaker close-up and slides, with titles and lower thirds | 2–3 cameras, a vision mixer, an operator for each role |
| **Interactive hybrid event** | Online speakers and viewers take part in Q&A, and remote presenters appear on the venue screen | Produced stream plus a link to the meeting platform, and a moderator |
| **Internal town hall** | Streamed to staff only, often on Teams or Zoom, with security controls | Produced stream to a private platform |

The more interaction you want between the room and online viewers, the more planning and crew you need.

---

## Choosing a platform

- **Zoom or Microsoft Teams**: best for **internal events** and interactive sessions. Viewers can join with existing work accounts, and Q&A tools are built in.
- **YouTube Live or LinkedIn Live**: best for **public events** and reaching a wider audience. Viewers don't need to register, and the recording stays online afterwards.
- **Event platforms**: useful for **paid or registered conferences** that need ticketing, multiple tracks and sponsor booths.

**Decide early.** Each platform has its own setup steps, account permissions and limits. LinkedIn Live, for example, requires access to be set up on the company page in advance.

---

## Internet: the most common point of failure

- **Use a wired connection.** Ask the venue for a **dedicated wired line** for the stream, not shared guest Wi-Fi.
- **Check the upload speed.** Test it at the venue during the site visit. Upload speed matters far more than download speed for streaming.
- **Have a backup.** A bonded or separate **4G/5G connection** that the stream can switch to if the main line drops.
- **Keep other traffic off the line.** Delegates' devices should use guest Wi-Fi, not the streaming connection.

A short test at the venue a few days before the event prevents most streaming problems.

## Audio and slides

### Audio
Online viewers only hear what the stream sends them. Take a **direct feed from the AV desk**, and check it during the rehearsal. Make sure:
- every microphone on stage is in the feed
- audience questions are picked up, with a handheld microphone or a dedicated question microphone
- videos played in the room are also sent to the stream with sound

### Slides
Online viewers need to see slides clearly. The best results come from taking the **slide output directly** from the presentation laptop or the AV switcher, and showing slides and speaker side by side or switching between them. Filming the projection screen with a camera is much harder to read.

---

## Rehearsal and run-of-show

- **Technical rehearsal**: test cameras, audio, slides, internet and the platform before doors open, ideally the day before for large events.
- **Speaker check**: test every remote presenter's connection, microphone and camera ahead of time.
- **Holding slide**: show a "starting soon" screen with music before the stream begins, so early viewers know they are in the right place.
- **Breaks**: plan what online viewers see during breaks, such as a holding slide or a sponsor video.
- **Ending**: plan the closing slide and when the stream stops.

---

## Looking after the online audience

Online viewers are easy to forget when the room is busy. Assign someone to:

- **watch the chat** and answer technical questions
- **collect online questions** and pass them to the moderator on stage
- **share links and materials** during the event
- **check the stream** on a separate device, to see exactly what viewers see

---

## Recording, privacy and permissions

- **Record the stream** and a clean local copy, for on-demand viewing and editing afterwards.
- **Tell attendees the event is being streamed,** on signage and in registration details. Audience members may appear on camera during Q&A. See our [INTERNAL LINK: /insights/pdpa-event-photography-consent-guide "PDPA consent guide for event photography and video"].
- **Get speaker permission** for streaming and recording, especially for external speakers.
- **Check music and video rights**: songs and clips played in the room can get public streams muted or taken down.
- **Restrict internal streams** to staff accounts, and turn off public recording links.

## FAQ

### What do we need to live stream a corporate event?
A wired internet connection with a mobile backup, an audio feed from the venue's AV desk, a way to combine slides and speakers, a technical rehearsal, and someone to manage the online chat and questions.

### Which platform should we use to live stream an event?
Use Zoom or Microsoft Teams for internal and interactive events, YouTube Live or LinkedIn Live for public events, and an event platform for paid or registered conferences with multiple tracks.

### Can we use the venue's Wi-Fi for live streaming?
It is risky. Ask for a dedicated wired line for the stream, test the upload speed at the venue, and have a 4G or 5G backup connection.

### How do online viewers ask questions during a hybrid event?
Assign a person to collect questions from the chat or Q&A tool and pass them to the moderator on stage, who reads them out so both audiences hear them.

### Can the live stream be recorded?
Yes. Record the stream and a separate local copy. The recording can be edited into session videos and a highlight reel after the event.
